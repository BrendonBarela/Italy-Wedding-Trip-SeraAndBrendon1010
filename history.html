History along our route

All of the fun history that used to live inside the destination pages is collected here, with extra facts for the places we'll actually walk through—from Roman Verona and Farnese Parma to Ispra's lime kilns, Ligurian sailing towns and the winter-resort Riviera.

Oct 1–4

Verona

Open city guide →

The context behind the streets

Verona works especially well if you know what survives underneath the postcard: a Roman forum, an imperial gate, a medieval dynasty, a rebuilt Roman bridge and a 20th-century Juliet myth layered onto a real medieval house.

Roman Verona1st century BCE onward • street grid, gates, Ponte Pietra and the Arena
Scaliger Verona13th–14th centuries • Della Scala rule, Castelvecchio, fortified bridges and Dante
Venetian rule1405–1797 • Verona becomes a key fortified city of the Republic of Venice
Austrian era19th century • military importance continues under the Habsburgs

UNESCO2000 • historic Verona is inscribed as a World Heritage Site
🏟️ The Arena was built for crowds

Built around the middle of the 1st century AD, the Arena could hold roughly 30,000 spectators and originally stood outside the Roman walls so huge event crowds did not clog the city center.

🧭 Piazza delle Erbe is the Roman forum

The oldest square in Verona occupies the site of the Roman forum—the political, commercial and social center of ancient Verona. So when we wander the market square, we are still standing in the city's original civic core.

🚪 Porta Borsari was the main Roman entrance

The surviving monumental façade dates to the 1st century AD. In the imperial city it was the principal gateway and was originally called Porta Iovia after a nearby temple of Jupiter.

🌉 Ponte Pietra is ancient and reconstructed

The bridge site is Roman, but retreating German troops destroyed the structure in 1945. Verona rebuilt it using stones and bricks recovered from the Adige.

🏰 Castelvecchio's bridge was also an escape route

Cangrande II della Scala built the fortified bridge in the 1350s partly so he could flee toward Tyrol if political enemies or a city revolt turned against him.

📖 Dante really lived in Verona

After exile from Florence, Dante spent years in Verona under Scaliger patronage. Cangrande della Scala became an important patron, and Verona is closely connected with the writing and dedication of parts of the Paradiso.

🔔 Lamberti's bells had civic jobs

The tower's medieval bells were not decorative: the Marangona marked work time and warned of fire; the Rengo called the city council and summoned citizens to arms.

❤️ The balcony is a 20th-century addition

Juliet's house is genuinely medieval, but the famous balcony was added during the 1939–40 restoration. The 2026 visitor route now leans into that distinction by starting inside Teatro Nuovo with interpretation of the Shakespeare myth.

🎼 The Arena's opera tradition is much younger than the building

The Roman amphitheatre is nearly two millennia old, but the famous open-air opera tradition began in 1913 with Verdi's Aida, staged for the centenary of the composer's birth.

🧱 The Arena spent centuries being recycled

After Rome, parts of the outer ring were dismantled for fortifications and the monument was used as a source of building stone. The surviving “Ala” is only a fragment of the original outer façade.

🏛️ Verona has one of Europe's oldest public lapidary museums

The Maffeiano Lapidary Museum was established in 1745. Its collections of Greek, Etruscan, Roman and other inscriptions grew from the work of Veronese scholar Scipione Maffei.

🕳️ There is another Verona under the streets

The Scaliger Excavations preserve Roman houses, mosaics, roads, drainage and later Lombard and medieval layers beneath the modern center—a literal cross-section through about two thousand years of city life.

Oct 4–7

Parma

Open city guide →

Food, art and court ambition

Parma makes more sense when the food and monuments are treated as the same story: Roman streets, medieval sculpture, Farnese spectacle, Correggio's illusionism, Maria Luigia's court and a modern food economy recognized by UNESCO.

Roman Parma183 BCE • Roman colony on the Via Emilia
Medieval city11th–13th c. • Cathedral, Antelami and the Baptistery
Farnese Duchy1545–1731 • Pilotta and court culture
Maria Luigia1816–1847 • Napoleon's second wife rules Parma
United Italy1860s • Duchy becomes part of Italy
Food City2015 • UNESCO Creative City of Gastronomy
🏛️ Parma's Roman core still shapes the center

Parma began as a Roman colony in the 2nd century BCE. The Via Emilia and the old civic center help explain why the modern historic core is so compact and walkable.

⛪ Antelami's Baptistery bridges two architectural worlds

Benedetto Antelami began the Baptistery in 1196. Its pink Verona marble and sculptural program sit right at the transition from Romanesque to early Gothic architecture.

🎨 Correggio made the Cathedral dome look open to heaven

The Assumption fresco, painted in the 1520s, uses radical foreshortening and swirling figures that later Baroque painters studied and imitated.

🎭 Teatro Farnese was built for a guest who never arrived

The theater was prepared for a planned visit by Cosimo II de' Medici, but the visit was cancelled. It was finally inaugurated in 1628 for a Medici-Farnese wedding.

🌊 The Farnese once flooded their own theater

For the 1628 inauguration, the theater hosted spectacular court entertainment that included a staged naval battle in a flooded arena—an expensive reason it was used only rarely.

💣 What we see is also a postwar reconstruction

Allied bombing in 1944 devastated Teatro Farnese. The postwar rebuilding reused surviving original material where possible, so today's space is both 17th-century theater and restoration story.

👑 Maria Luigia reshaped Parma after Napoleon

Napoleon's second wife ruled Parma from 1816 to 1847 and became deeply associated with the city's civic works, Teatro Regio and the violets that still carry her name.

🧀 Parma's food identity is officially part of its modern history

UNESCO named Parma a Creative City of Gastronomy in 2015, recognizing a food culture that is still central to the region's economy as well as its tourism.

🎶 Maria Luigia gave Parma its great opera house

The Teatro Regio began as the Nuovo Teatro Ducale at the request of Duchess Maria Luigia. It opened on May 16, 1829 with Bellini's Zaira.

🔥 The opera house had heating hidden under the foyer

The Teatro Regio's foyer floor still shows trapdoors through which heat was distributed—an unexpectedly practical detail inside one of Italy's most elegant opera houses.

🪵 Teatro Farnese is theatrical illusion all the way down

The enormous court theater was built rapidly from lightweight materials such as wood and painted plaster. Its monumental stone-like appearance is itself a piece of stagecraft.

📚 The 1944 bombing hit Parma's books as well as its theater

Bombing of the Pilotta damaged the Farnese Theatre and Palatine Library; roughly 15,000 books were lost. The theater was later reconstructed, with surviving original fragments put back into the rebuilt structure.

Oct 7–11

Ispra

Open city guide →

The lake before the wedding

Ispra's real story is local: medieval power struggles, a strategic border, limestone extraction and lime furnaces. The grand Borromean-resort story belongs more to Angera and Stresa—which is exactly why those work as contrasting excursions.

1276Torriani and Visconti forces fight near the Quassera

📜 Ispra is documented at least as early as 826

The town's municipal history page cites an 826 parchment now kept in Milan's State Archive as the oldest known document naming Ispra.

⛪ Medieval Ispra had a surprising number of churches

By the 13th century, records indicate seven churches or chapels in the settlement, reflecting the area's strong religious life.

⚔️ A power struggle for Milan reached Ispra

In 1276 a bloody battle near the Quassera involved the Torriani and supporters of Archbishop Ottone Visconti in the struggle for control of Milan.

🏰 The Borromeo connection is older than the tourist sites

In the early modern period Ispra formed part of the Borromeo family's feudal territory—the same family still associated with major Lake Maggiore landmarks.

🛂 Ispra once sat on a meaningful border

Under Austrian rule it hosted a customs station because the area lay on the frontier between Lombardy and Piedmont.

🔥 Lime—not tourism—shaped much of the waterfront

From the 19th into the early 20th century, local limestone was quarried and burned in numerous furnaces. Several survive along the lakeshore today.

💍 Villa Eden literally sits on that industrial history

The official property description says Villa Eden stands on the remains of a 19th-century furnace; its distinctive octagonal roofs echo the form of the old kilns.

🌊 The lakefront is longer and wilder than the town center suggests

The Comune notes that much of Ispra's shoreline can be walked, with old docks, quarries, furnaces, small harbors and natural areas appearing along the route.

⚛️ Italy's first research reactor was built in Ispra

The Ispra-1 research reactor was completed and inaugurated in 1959. It became a landmark in the early history of Italian and European nuclear research.

🇪🇺 Ispra became part of the European science project almost immediately

The Euratom Treaty of 1957 called for a Joint Nuclear Research Centre, and the Ispra site was formally launched in 1959. Today's European Commission Joint Research Centre grew from that postwar push for shared research.

🔬 The town's modern identity is unusually international

The JRC brought scientists and technical staff from across Europe to a small Lake Maggiore town, adding a distinctly European research layer to Ispra's older fishing, quarrying and border history.

Oct 11–14

Santa Margherita, Portofino & Camogli

Open city guide →

Three towns, three personalities

Santa, Portofino and Camogli look like one postcard coastline today, but they grew out of fishing, maritime trade, defensive forts and very different relationships with tourism.

Roman tracesSanta has evidence suggesting settlement in Roman times
Medieval coastFishing villages, Saracen raids and defensive castles
Genoese worldMaritime trade ties the coast to the Republic of Genoa
1813Santa's villages briefly unified as “Porto Napoleone”
Late 1800sRoad + railway launch Riviera tourism
Modern RivieraSanta becomes the practical base; Portofino the icon; Camogli preserves seafaring identity
🏘️ Santa Margherita wasn't always one town

The area developed around separate villages including Pescino and Corte. In 1813, under Napoleon, they were unified as “Porto Napoleone”; the modern Santa Margherita name was confirmed later in the 19th century.

🌿 Villa Durazzo began as a summer estate

The Durazzo family built the villa in 1678. Its gardens, sea loggia and later grand-hotel history capture Santa's shift from coastal town to elegant resort.

🚂 The railway changed Santa's destiny

The railway and new road toward Portofino opened the coastline to travelers and triggered the age of grand hotels and villas that still defines Santa's look.

🚤 Portofino was genuinely isolated

Until the late 19th century, Portofino could essentially be reached only by sea or difficult hill paths. A carriage road finally began breaking that isolation in the 1880s.

🏰 An English consul helped make Portofino fashionable

Montagu Brown acquired the old San Giorgio fortress—today Castello Brown—and his hospitality helped draw British and Genoese high society to what had been a fishing village.

⛵ Camogli was the “Town of a Thousand White Sailboats”

Its 17th–19th century maritime tradition produced shipowners and trading vessels far beyond what the village's small size suggests today.

🎨 Camogli's facades are part architecture, part illusion

Like much of Liguria, buildings use trompe-l'oeil painted architectural details—false cornices, stonework and even shutters—to make tall coastal facades more elaborate.

🙏 Madonna dei Gotti is a sailors' tradition

The Camogli festival centers on a votive shrine to the Madonna of the Good Voyage, protector of fishermen and sailors; the “Gotti” nickname recalls the old taverns around the harbor.

🏺 Santa has a Roman clue sitting inside its basilica

A 3rd-century cinerary urn with a Latin inscription, found locally and preserved in the Basilica of Santa Margherita, is one of the pieces of evidence for settlement here in Roman times.

🛤️ Portofino's first public road transport was horse-drawn

After the new carriage road reduced the village's isolation, a public horse-drawn coach service began in 1888. Before that, bad weather could make the hill paths effectively unusable.

🎭 Camogli's theater was funded by families who got rich at sea

The Teatro Sociale opened in 1876 after an initiative by 60 local bourgeois families whose fortunes came from Camogli's maritime economy.

🏺 Camogli's story goes back far beyond its sailing-ship boom

Archaeological finds from the Castellaro area include material dated to the 12th–13th centuries BCE, plus later Roman-period evidence of trade by road and sea.

⚓ Camogli preserved the paperwork of a maritime powerhouse

Its maritime museum holds navigation objects, ship models and manuscripts including the 1853 statutes of Camogli's Maritime Mutual Insurance, along with letters connected to Garibaldi and the Expedition of the Thousand.

Oct 14–18

Beaulieu, Èze & Nice

Open city guide →

Why this coast feels so layered

This stretch of the Riviera compresses ancient Greece, medieval Savoy, Victorian winter tourism and Belle Époque fantasy architecture into a few train stops.

Ancient coastGreek and Roman settlement leave traces around Nice and Beaulieu
Medieval ÈzeFortified eagle's-nest village above the sea
Savoy / SardiniaNice remains tied to the House of Savoy for centuries
1860Nice becomes definitively French
Belle ÉpoqueBeaulieu and Nice flourish as winter resorts
2021Nice's winter-resort heritage enters UNESCO's World Heritage List
🏖️ Beaulieu was once just a small hamlet

It became an independent commune in 1891. The Belle Époque transformed it into a fashionable winter resort for royalty, wealthy European families and artists. Its motto is Pax in pulchritudine—“peace in beauty.”

🏛️ Kérylos is Belle Époque architecture pretending to be ancient Greece—in the best way

Built from 1902–1908, it recreates the atmosphere of a noble Delos house from around the 2nd century BC while quietly integrating early-20th-century comfort.

🌸 Villa Ephrussi is really nine gardens, not one

The pink Rothschild villa on Cap-Ferrat is surrounded by French, Spanish, Florentine, Japanese, exotic, Provençal and other themed gardens overlooking the sea.

🏰 Èze's best viewpoint sits in a destroyed fortress

The Exotic Garden occupies the summit ruins of the medieval castle, around 429 m above the Mediterranean. The military site became a cactus-and-succulent garden with one of the coast's widest views.

🧠 Nietzsche's name is attached to a genuinely steep trail

The path between Èze-sur-Mer and the village climbs roughly 350 m. Nietzsche stayed on the Riviera in the 1880s, and local tradition connects the trail with his work on the third part of Thus Spoke Zarathustra.

🇮🇹 Nice's “Italian” feel is historical, not decorative

Before 1860, Nice had long belonged to the House of Savoy and then the Kingdom of Sardinia. France annexed the County of Nice in 1860 after the Treaty of Turin and a popular vote.

🇬🇧 The Promenade des Anglais really does come from the English

In 1824, British Reverend Lewis Way encouraged construction of a seaside path that also provided work for local residents after a difficult winter. It evolved into today's nearly 7 km promenade.

🌴 UNESCO protects Nice partly because tourism changed the city

Nice joined the World Heritage List in 2021 as the “Winter Resort Town of the Riviera,” recognizing the architecture, gardens, promenades and urban form created by centuries of international winter visitors.

📐 Nice was deliberately redesigned for winter visitors

In 1832, while Nice was still in the Kingdom of Sardinia, the Consiglio d'Ornato created planning and architectural rules meant to make the city more attractive to foreign residents and visitors.

🚂 The railway helped turn Nice into an international resort

After Nice became French in 1860, connection to the European rail network accelerated the arrival of winter visitors and the construction of new districts beyond the medieval old town.

🏨 A Beaulieu hotel advertised cutting-edge plumbing and an elevator in 1885

The Palais des Anglais was among the town's early luxury hotels; local Belle Époque history notes that it was an early adopter of toilets and a hydraulic lift—technology worth advertising to wealthy winter guests.

🗼 Gustave Eiffel became a Beaulieu homeowner

After members of the Eiffel family stayed at Villa Durandy in the 1890s, Gustave Eiffel liked the house enough to buy it. The residence became part of Beaulieu's extraordinary Belle Époque roll call.

❄️ The Riviera was a winter destination before it was a summer one

Nice's UNESCO story centers on winter tourism from the mid-18th century through the 1930s. Only later did the image of the Riviera shift decisively toward summer beaches and seaside holidays.
